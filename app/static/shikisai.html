Eighteen runs at Shikisai

Shikisai Sprint is the road my friend FishFighterNS and I have started more than any other in Forza Horizon 6: 28 times between us. Here are the 18 runs we finished in the same car, a 2016 Aston Martin Vulcan, all leaving the line at once.

By Captn Compass

Ghost race

FishFighterNS · 3 runs Captn Compass · 15 runs

    0:00.000

    They were driven on different days, so nobody could see anybody else. It’s a ghost race. Places come from distance along the road, as on the Replay page, which can show up to six runs with speed, gear and pedals.

    The night shift

    Fourteen runs in 36 minutes. Then he drove it twice.

    On the evening of 4 November I drove Shikisai fourteen times back to back. My first run was 2:09.405. By the end I’d found about two seconds: my first seven runs averaged 2:08.6 and my last seven 2:06.5. My best, 2:02.493, came on run thirteen.

    About nine hours later FishFighterNS sat down and drove it twice. 2:02.911, then 2:00.718. He beat my whole evening on his second try, by 1.775 seconds.

    How we drive

    Same grip, different right foot

    We corner equally hard. In both our runs the car peaks at about 3.3 g sideways, so that’s the car’s limit, not ours.

    The throttle is different. Mine is nearly always fully on or fully off: on any run, the time I spend above 90% is the same as the time I spend above 50%. His sits in between for more than a tenth of every run, easing it. That could be how he drives or what he drives with: a controller and a keyboard don’t press the same way, and the data can’t tell those apart.

    Why keep your own data

    The game gives you a time. Then it forgets the run.

    Forza tells you your time and where you placed. It doesn’t keep the fourteen runs from one evening, or how you took each corner. But the game has a setting, Data Out under Settings → HUD and Gameplay, that sends what the car is doing, about sixty times a second, to any computer you point it at.

    SplitTime is that computer. Since 12 October it has caught 572 sessions, about 15.6 hours of driving and 2.7 GB of readings, all on a small server I rent. Because the data is ours, we can ask questions the game never will. Which of my runs was best, and where did it gain? Did I get better over an evening? Who’s faster where? This page is one answer. The same 18 runs went out to Blender, a free 3D program, as a few files in a couple of minutes. Here’s the whole race, from the start until the last of my cars gets home.

    How I made the films from our runs

    SplitTime saved where the car was, which way it was pointing and what we were doing with the pedals, about sixty times a second. Each run became a little sports car in Blender. I lined up the starts and smoothed the movement over a few readings so the cars wouldn’t twitch between frames.

    The brake pedal lights the rear lamps. Where a tire slips under braking, it leaves a mark. The engine sound comes from the recorded revs and throttle. It changes as each car gets closer to the camera and then disappears down the road.

    The game gave us the path we drove. I built the road around it and added the hills, trees and cameras. That’s how I could put a camera in the middle of Corner 2 after both of us had finished driving.

    How I made the announcer

    The voice is Mark, a preset voice in Runway’s speech generation, using the Eleven v4 model (eleven_v4). It was made in two takes from the prompts below. The words in square brackets are delivery cues, not spoken. The second take was given the last sentence of the first as context, so it carries on in the same voice. I edited the calls to match the recorded positions.

    Take 1 · Launch and approach

    [excited] And they're away at Shikisai! Eighteen runs in the same Aston Martin Vulcan, all leaving the line together. [pause] Captain Compass in amber. Look for Fish in teal. [pause] Three hundred kilometres an hour! Now he's on the brakes for the first sweeper. [pause] Back on the power. Watch that teal car into turn two.

    Take 2 · Corner 2

    [excited] Fish in fifth on the way in. [pause] Fourth! Third! [pause] He's alongside! Second! [excited] Fish takes the lead!

    The engines aren’t part of the voice: they’re synthesized separately from each car’s recorded telemetry, and the voice drops out at 0:32 for the flyby, leaving only the engines until the clip ends at 0:44.5.

    Who maintains it?

    I do, the same way I built it: in plain English

    I don’t read the code. I describe what I want, AI coding assistants write it, and each change arrives as a short pull request with a plain description and a line on the What’s new page. Since 12 October that’s 71 changes and 41 written-down design decisions. When something’s wrong I say so in a sentence. The cards beside the Replay used to jump when places changed; I mentioned it, and now they slide.

    Is the code sloppy? Maybe. I’ll never look. It does exactly what the two of us need, and it doesn’t have to do anything else. It isn’t flawless. In October the website part once grew to 5.5 GB of memory in under an hour and slowed the whole server. I still don’t know why. It now has a hard limit and restarts itself if it reaches it, and recordings carry on regardless.

    None of this is a new idea. Robin Sloan made a messaging app for his family alone and wrote that “when you liberate programming from the requirement to be professional and scalable, it becomes a different activity altogether” (An app can be a home-cooked meal, 2020). Clay Shirky called it situated software, “designed for use by a specific social group, rather than for a generic set of ‘users’” (Situated Software, 2004). He was honest about the cost: it gets brittle if lots more people show up. That’s fine. There are two of us.
